# Luna

A small, cute isometric 3D game. Luna, a labrador puppy, explores a sandy park and tries to make friends with the other dogs. Built with Godot 4 and models generated by Blender scripts.

**Why this exists:** Luna is a teaching example. It shows how someone with no coding background, child or adult, can turn an idea into a playable game by describing it to an AI coding assistant ([Claude Code](https://claude.com/claude-code)). Every line of code, every 3D model and every sound here came from conversation. The guide below explains how.

## How this game was made: a prompting guide

### Start with one good prompt
The whole game began with a single paragraph. You don't need technical words. You need a clear picture of what you want to *play*. A good first prompt covers:

| Ingredient | What it means | In Luna's prompt |
|---|---|---|
| **The idea in one line** | What is it? | "A simple isometric 3D game called Luna" |
| **The hero and the goal** | Who do you play, and what are you trying to do? | "A labrador puppy trying to befriend other dogs" |
| **The rules** | How do you win, and what gets in the way? | "+1 for each dog she touches, −1 if an aggressive dog touches her, win at 10, no losing" |
| **Characters with personality** | What makes things interesting? | "Timid dogs run away, some stay still, aggressive ones chase" |
| **A look and feel** | A reference everyone knows | "Simple and cute, like Animal Crossing" |
| **Controls** | Keep them simple | "WASD" |
| **Where it runs** | Your computer | "Runs on Windows" |
| **Boundaries** | What *not* to do (yet) | "Future ideas: energy, food… Don't increase the scope unduly" |
| **Ask me first** | Let the AI check its understanding | "Ask questions and propose gameplay suggestions" |

**A one-shot prompt you can copy and adapt:**

> I want to build a simple game that runs on my computer. It's called **[name]**. You play as **[hero]**, who **[goal]**.
> Rules: **[how you score, how you win, what gets in the way]**. There's no way to lose.
> Other characters: **[2–3 kinds, each with a different personality and behaviour]**.
> Style: simple and cute, like **[a game or film everyone knows]**. Controls: **[e.g. WASD to move]**.
> The world is **[a simple setting, e.g. a park with sandy paths and fenced grass]**.
> Later I might add **[future ideas]**, but not now.
> Before you build anything, ask me questions and suggest gameplay ideas, but keep the first version small. Then make a plan, build it, and check it actually works by playing it yourself.

Why this works: it describes the *experience*, not the code. It gives a style reference instead of a long description. It sets a scope boundary, so the first version is small and finished rather than big and broken. And "ask me questions first" catches misunderstandings before any work is done.

### Then build it up, one conversation at a time (multi-shot)
After the first version, Luna grew through many short, plain-English requests. They fall into a few patterns you can reuse:

1. **Answer its questions, then approve a plan.** The assistant asked about the game engine, art style and scoring, and offered choices with a recommendation. Picking the recommended options was fine. Reviewing the plan before building stops surprises.
2. **Play it, then react to what you see or feel.** *"I like the look and feel, but scale it up to 200% to ramp up the cute factor."* You don't need to know *how*. Describe the feeling you want.
3. **Ask for it to be adjustable.** *"Can this be a configurable change?"* Now the zoom is something players control, rather than a fixed guess.
4. **Ask "what could go wrong?" and let it point out side effects.** Zooming in meant dogs reacted off-screen. The assistant raised it, suggested two fixes, and the answer was simply *"I like both, can they be implemented?"*
5. **Add one mechanic at a time, described as a rule.** *"Make bark scare away aggressive dogs."* Later: *"We can befriend an aggressive dog in a short window after we bark at them."* Each one small, testable and fun to try.
6. **Polish what's already there.** *"Put the text labels in speech bubbles."* *"Rather than jump straight into the game, add a splash screen."* Small requests, big difference in how it feels.
7. **Ask for opinions, not just changes.** *"Could [some new AI tool] be used for the dog behaviour, or is it not appropriate?"* A good assistant will tell you when an idea isn't a good fit, and explain why.
8. **Ask it to brainstorm, then choose.** *"Peeing and pooping next: suggest some gameplay ideas."* *"How can we make this game more sticky?"* You get a menu of options, and you stay in charge of the choices.
9. **Ask it to prove things work.** *"Can you confirm that the peeing works?"* This found a real gap: pressing the key before Luna needed to go did nothing, with no feedback. So we added a "Not now!" bubble.
10. **Look after the project.** *"Record the decisions in ADRs." "Push this to the git repo." "Add a CLAUDE.md and a ship skill." "Write the specs and commit them, I want to do something else first."* Saving the project, writing down decisions and planning ahead are all things you can just ask for.

### Tips that made the biggest difference
- **Describe feelings and rules, not code.** "Cuter", "too hard", "the dogs feel sluggish" are all useful.
- **Give references.** "Like Animal Crossing" carries a whole art style in three words.
- **Keep each request small.** One idea per message is easier to check and easier to undo.
- **Say what you *don't* want.** "Don't increase the scope unduly" kept the first version finishable.
- **Let it ask questions.** Then you only answer the ones that matter to you.
- **Play after every change, and say what you notice.** You're the playtester, and that's the most important job.
- **Ask "does it work?" and "what could go wrong?"** It will check its own work and point out problems before you find them.
- **Save as you go.** "Commit and push" is your save button, so nothing is ever lost.

### Trying the same game with other AI models and tools
Want to compare? Use the **same one-shot prompt** with different assistants, then the same few follow-ups, and score each result against the same checklist: Does it run? Do the controls work? Are all three dog personalities there? Does the score work, with no going below zero? Does it look cute? How many messages did it take?

What to change in the prompt depends mostly on **whether the assistant can run things on your computer**:

- **AI coding agents that can work on your computer** (Claude Code, OpenAI's Codex, Gemini's command-line tool and similar). They can create files, run tools and test the game themselves, so the one-shot prompt works almost as-is. Add: *"Run the game and check it works before telling me it's done."* Results will vary between models, and that's the interesting part of the experiment.
- **Chat assistants in a browser** (Claude.ai, ChatGPT, Gemini). They can't install Godot or Blender for you. Add: *"Make it a single web page I can open in my browser, using three.js for the 3D, with simple shapes for the dogs."* This is the easiest route for a classroom: nothing to install, and you can play it straight away. Some chat apps can even show the game right next to the conversation.

**Changing the game engine:**

| Tool | Good for | Add to the prompt |
|---|---|---|
| **Godot** (used here) | Free, lightweight, and an AI can write almost all of it as text | *"Use Godot 4 and build the scenes in code so I don't need the editor."* |
| **Unity** | Popular and powerful, with lots of tutorials | *"Use Unity. Write the C# scripts, and either an editor script that builds the scene for me or step-by-step instructions for setting it up in the Unity editor. Use simple built-in shapes for the dogs."* Expect more clicking in the Unity editor yourself. |
| **Web / three.js** | No installs; share it as a link | *"One HTML file with three.js. Cute rounded shapes, pastel colours."* |
| **Roblox Studio** | Kids already know it and can share with friends | *"Make it for Roblox Studio in Luau. Tell me exactly where each script goes."* |
| **Python + Pygame** | Simple 2D version for learning to code | *"Make a 2D top-down version with Pygame, with cute coloured circles for the dogs."* |

**For the 3D characters:** an agent that can run Blender can generate models with scripts, as Luna's were. Otherwise, ask for *"simple rounded shapes built in code"*, or *"use free CC0 models from Kenney or Quaternius, and tell me where to download them."*

**Tips for a fair comparison:** use exactly the same first prompt, keep the follow-ups the same, and write down what each assistant asked you and how many messages it took to get something playable. The differences in *how* they work (questions asked, testing, explanations) are as interesting as the game you end up with.

## How to play
The game opens on a title screen: press **Play** (or Enter/Space) to start, Esc to quit.

- **WASD / arrows**: move · **Shift**: sneak · **Space**: bark (scares off nearby aggressive dogs) · **E**: go potty · **Mouse wheel / + −**: zoom (default 200%, remembered) · **Esc**: pause
- Touch a friendly or timid dog to befriend it: **+1** (each dog only counts once). Aggressive dogs can be befriended too, but only just after you bark at them.
- Aggressive dogs (Dobermans and German Shepherds in the gated yards) chase Luna. If one tags her: **−1**. The score never goes below 0.
- Reach **10** friends to win.
- **Energy** drains as Luna moves around (and when she barks). Below 25% she's slow and droopy. Walk into one of the 6 **food bowls** to eat: +40 energy. Bowls refill after 30 s.
- After 2 bowls Luna needs to **pee**, and after 4 to **poop**. Press **E** to go: on grass (the lawns and yards) she gets a "Good girl!". Wait more than 20 s and she has an accident.
- **Pee spots lure timid dogs.** They come over to sniff and stay calm while sniffing, so it's a good moment to befriend them.

| Personality | Breeds | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Friendly | Golden Retriever, Corgi, Beagle, Pug | Wander, then stop and wag when Luna comes close |
| Timid | Chihuahua, Whippet, Dachshund, Poodle | Run away when Luna is within 8 m (3 m when sneaking), or 6 m and 2.25 m at the default zoom. After a few seconds of running they get tired and sit down, which is your chance to catch them |
| Aggressive | Doberman, German Shepherd | Chase Luna within 7 m of their yard. After a tag they lose interest for 5 s. Bark at one and it cowers and runs off: catch it within 3 s to befriend it (+1) |

Notice ranges shrink as you zoom in (75% at the default 200%), so dogs react near the edge of the screen. A "!" pops up when a dog notices Luna. Tuning (speeds, ranges, timings) is in `game/scripts/breeds.gd`.

## Project layout
- `tools/blender/`: Blender scripts that build every model (`build_all.sh`).
- `tools/audio/make_sounds.py`: synthesises the sound effects and music (standard library only).
- `game/`: the Godot project. The world, dogs and HUD are built in code from `scenes/Main.tscn`.
- `game/tests/test_gameplay.tscn`: headless gameplay checks.

## Commands
Shortcuts: `make run` · `make test` · `make assets` · `make windows` · `make clean`

```sh
GODOT=/Applications/Godot.app/Contents/MacOS/Godot

# Rebuild assets (only needed after editing the generators)
tools/blender/build_all.sh [--preview lineup.png]
python3 tools/audio/make_sounds.py

# Play
$GODOT --path game

# Run the gameplay tests (exit code 0 = pass)
$GODOT --headless --path game res://tests/test_gameplay.tscn

# Export the Windows build -> build/Luna.exe
# (needs Godot 4.7.2 export templates installed)
mkdir -p build && $GODOT --headless --path game --export-release "Windows Desktop" ../build/Luna.exe
```

Debug flags (after `--`; any flag skips the title screen unless `--splash` is given): `--seed=N`, `--luna-pos=x,z`, `--zoom=1.5`, `--cam-size=48`, `--demo`, `--needs-demo[=poop]`, `--splash [--autoplay]`, `--screenshot=out.png --shot-delay=2`.
